# Sources and reading boundaries

[Contents](README.md) · [Concept index](INDEX.md)

## What this bibliography establishes

This edition synthesizes an initial abstraction study, a fuller methodological distillation, its annotated source library, and subsequent explanatory developments. The studies and the relevant Grothendieck portion of the source library were read for the synthesis. The source ranges below describe the reading reported in those records, rather than claiming a fresh reading of every original during preparation of this edition.

The reference preserves bibliographic identity, exact public routes, consequential passage locations, and attribution limits. It includes authored explanations rather than full copies of source texts. Public accessibility does not establish a redistribution license.

Source keys in the chapters distinguish historical grounding from proposed general practice. They do not mean that the author of the cited source endorsed every adaptation. English descriptions of French material are paraphrases unless explicitly marked as translated quotations.

## P: Promenade through a work

Alexandre Grothendieck, *Promenade à travers une œuvre, ou L'enfant et la Mère*, January 1986.

[Original French PDF](https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~leila.schneps/grothendieckcircle/prom.pdf).

Reported reading covers §§1-15, the opening of §16, and §§17-18. PDF page positions differ from the original P-markers. The following locations use PDF pages unless both systems are specified.

- §§1-2, pp. 2-5: questions about the nature of length, area, and volume; independent work that substantially rediscovered existing measure and integration theory. The methodological use is independent understanding with respect for prior knowledge.
- §3, note 6, pp. 6-7: a correction is preserved as a later correction instead of being retrojected into the original perception.
- §§5-9, pp. 9-21: complementary roles and viewpoints; detailed shaping and assembly; the relation of themes to a whole; understanding through expression; the danger of imposing a plan before examining the subject.
- §6, pp. 11-13, corresponding to P15-P17: overlooked questions, missing concepts, appropriate statements, and illuminating viewpoints.
- §11, pp. 24-26: humility and audacity; the detailed development needed to show that a new language expresses an intuition and recovers or extends known results.
- §§12-13, pp. 26-31, notes 43-50: limits of the Zariski topology for the intended cohomological work; spaces studied through sheaves; categories of set-valued sheaves as carriers of spatial structure; movement toward a broader setting. Note 46 distinguishes set-valued sheaves from abelian coefficient sheaves. Note 47 qualifies reconstruction of spaces by sobriety, including Hausdorff spaces. The passage emphasizes exactness but is not a full axiomatization of topoi.
- §14, pp. 31-32: the two-sided demand that a broader notion retain essential old intuitions and constructions while admitting previously excluded situations; years of apparatus-building and collaboration remain part of the account.
- §15 and opening of §16, p. 33: the imagery does not by itself add a transferable operation. The opening of §16 credits Deligne's concluding contribution to the Weil conjectures.
- §§17-18, pp. 37-41, especially note 64 on p. 39: receptive attention and constructive expression are intertwined. Reconsidering heroic building imagery does not erase the meticulous foundational work.

This is retrospective, partly philosophical self-description. It is not a causal study of discovery or proof that generalization always helps. Personal metaphors are not treated as psychological classifications.

## R: Récoltes et Semailles

Alexandre Grothendieck, *Récoltes et Semailles*, French transcription edited by Mateo Carmona.

[French transcription PDF](https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~leila.schneps/grothendieckcircle/RetSbis.pdf).

The opening identifies Carmona as transcriber/editor and the edition as provisional. References here use this transcription's PDF pages, not the original typescript pagination cited in some secondary works or a commercial edition.

Reported reading covers Part I §§1-9; Part III note 122, pp. 682-689; note 123, pp. 689-693; and the opening of note 124, pp. 693-694. The rest of the 1,502-page transcription was not claimed read.

- Part I §2, "Erreur et découverte," pp. 163-165: provisional assertions expose errors. An error may clarify an intuition, but an attractive overall picture may also be deeply false.
- Part I §§3-5, pp. 165-170, especially p. 169: polished exposition omits much of the original route; meticulous exposition remains indispensable craft and can itself produce discovery.
- Part I §6, "Le Rêveur," pp. 172-174, especially p. 173: conviction can precede proof while leaving substantial ingenuity and labor unresolved.
- Part I §8, "Rêve et démonstration," pp. 176-178: ripening and forceful problem-solving are contrasted, with acknowledgement that the latter can be used successfully.
- Part I §9, "L'étranger bienvenu," pp. 178-180: the welcome of Cartan and Leray and the ability to ask elementary questions without shame.
- Part III note 122, pp. 682-686: revision of an earlier interpretation of his own style; intuition and logical structure develop together; formalism driven mainly by internal coherence is contrasted with work animated by a concrete subject.
- Part III note 122, pp. 687-689: nut-softening and rising-sea passages; no advance guarantee of efficacy; fertility includes new questions, notions, and viewpoints. The cited problem settings include Riemann-Roch in arbitrary characteristic, prime-to-characteristic fundamental groups of curves, rationality of L-functions over finite fields, and semistable reduction of abelian varieties.
- Part III note 123, pp. 689-693: Serre's role in transmitting the mathematical substance and attraction of questions; complementary styles and explicit qualifications to an overly neat contrast between them.
- Opening of Part III note 124, pp. 693-694: unity through diversity without sacrificing its richness; following analogies toward common roots rather than flattening differences.

[DeepL Part III](https://www.ucw.cz/~ps/grothendieck/deepl/d_3_part3.pdf), pp. 87-94, and a [Google translation](https://www.ucw.cz/~ps/grothendieck/part.3.ch.18a.pdf), pp. 53-59, reportedly helped locate notes 122-123. They are machine translations, not independent authorities. The later source reading reported direct inspection of the French. The page systems must not be combined into a single citation.

No priority grievances or personal psychological speculations are adjudicated here.

## D: Deligne on principal ideas

Pierre Deligne, "Quelques idées maîtresses de l'œuvre de A. Grothendieck," *Séminaires et Congrès* 3 (1998), pp. 11-19, Société Mathématique de France.

[Publisher record](https://smf.emath.fr/publications/quelques-idees-maitresses-de-loeuvre-de-grothendieck) · [Scanned article](https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~leila.schneps/grothendieckcircle/Mathbiographies/Deligne.pdf).

The source library reports all nine pages read visually after text extraction failed. Printed page number equals PDF page position plus 10.

- Opening, pp. 11-12: the nut-softening passage from *Récoltes et Semailles*, plus an SGA 5 argument assembled from apparently modest reductions. Meticulous construction is part of the example.
- §1, pp. 12-13: affine schemes for all commutative unital rings; fiber products; the relative object $X\to S$ and change of base.
- §1, p. 14: generalized points and the fully faithful Yoneda embedding; a moduli problem as a functor; representability as a substantive question; credit to Artin's extension of the work.
- §2, pp. 15-16: descent of properties, morphisms, and objects; gluing and localization; étale topology; Galois descent and Serre's work as antecedents.
- §3, pp. 16-17: six operations across cohomological settings whose proofs can differ substantially.
- §4, pp. 17-18: motives and the family resemblance of cohomology theories; the original aim described as not attained in this 1998 account despite substantial influence.

The article is a concise retrospective by a principal participant. Its historical assessment of motives is not a present-status survey, and its mathematics does not establish empirical analogies outside the stated setting.

## C: Cartier's historical account

Pierre Cartier, "Alexander Grothendieck: A Country Known Only by Name," *Notices of the American Mathematical Society*, April 2015.

[DOI 10.1090/noti1235](https://doi.org/10.1090/noti1235) · [Ten-page PDF](https://pages.physics.ua.edu/staff/fabi/CT/rnoti-p373.pdf).

The source library reports inspection of the ten-page article, particularly "Homological Algebra" and "Algebraic Geometry and Arithmetic Geometry," PDF pp. 5-6; "The Great Problems," "The Method," and "Schemes," pp. 7-8; and "Toposes" and "Motives," pp. 9-10.

The account supplies concrete mathematical context for broader settings, products, base change, and the difference between ambition and realization. Psychological speculation is excluded from the methodological synthesis. This is an interpreter's historical account, not controlled causal evidence for a general success formula.

## M: McLarty and the rising sea

Colin McLarty, "The Rising Sea: Grothendieck on simplicity and generality I," manuscript dated 24 May 2003.

[24-page manuscript](https://ncatlab.org/nlab/files/McLartyRisingSea.pdf) · [Alternative copy](https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~leila.schneps/grothendieckcircle/Mathbiographies/mclarty1.pdf).

The later source library reports reading all 24 manuscript PDF pages, with the argument on pp. 1-22 and bibliography beginning on p. 22. An earlier study had relied on selected passages. Some extracted equations were corrupt and were not copied as authoritative formulas.

The [author's 2010 CV](https://www.colinmclarty.com/files/McLartyCV2010.pdf), PDF p. 3, identifies the published essay in Gray and Parshall, eds., *Episodes in the History of Recent Algebra* (AMS, 2007), pp. 301-326. The manuscript and published chapter are not assumed identical; page references in this collection use the manuscript.

- pp. 1-2: the rising-sea metaphor, quoted from *Récoltes et Semailles*. Footnote 1 identifies McLarty's translations. Quotations mediated through him are not independent French translations.
- pp. 5-11, especially 7-11: categorical exactness, abelian categories, injective resolutions, and common foundations for cohomology; special resolutions can remain useful for calculation.
- pp. 18-19, especially note 36: nilpotent structure invisible in all point residue fields.
- pp. 20-21: relative schemes, variation in families, and base change; different bases retain their own geometry.
- p. 21: representable functors and compatibility across test objects. This does not attribute invention of Yoneda's lemma to Grothendieck.
- p. 14 and pp. 16-17: sheaves and gluing; not an unrestricted effectiveness theorem for every descent datum.
- pp. 11-12, note 19; p. 17, note 30; pp. 21-22: a cohomology theory still needs the properties required for the intended arithmetic use; the hoped-for route and Deligne's eventual argument are distinguished.
- The discussion credits antecedents and contributions from Serre, Mac Lane, Buchsbaum, Baer, Godement, Dieudonné, and earlier algebraic geometers.

This is historical scholarship with draft features and some explicitly speculative historical explanation. It does not establish that every general theory will dissolve its motivating problem.

## E: Esquisse d'un Programme

Alexandre Grothendieck, *Esquisse d'un Programme* (1984), consulted in English as *Sketch of a Programme*.

[English PDF](https://www.landsburg.com/grothendieck/EsquisseEng.pdf) · [Publisher metadata](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511758874.017).

The publisher record identifies an English chapter in *Geometric Galois Actions* (1997), pp. 243-284. The consulted mirror's opening extraction did not identify the translator, so none is inferred. PDF page positions below are not assumed to match manuscript or published pagination.

Reported reliance is on §§1-3 and §5, particularly:

- §2, PDF p. 4: seeking a general language adequate to algebraic geometry, alongside the return to elementary surfaces and curves.
- §2, p. 5: coarse moduli and the loss of automorphism groups.
- §2, p. 6: suitable families of base points and fundamental groupoids retaining relevant symmetries.
- §3, pp. 10-13: finite embedded maps, ramified coverings, and algebraic curves over number fields; explicit credit to Belyi on p. 13.
- §5, p. 24: temporary plausible tame axioms and comparison theorems as a test.
- §5, p. 26: working through specific examples to distinguish right from wrong.
- §5, p. 27: direct geometric arguments that may shortcut machinery after the viewpoint has become clear.

This is a programme containing proposed directions, not a document in which every suggested result is claimed proved. No audit of the present status of its conjectures is supplied.

## What is explanatory rather than historically attributed

The dual-number calculation, the comparison of a point with a nonreduced point, the one-object groupoid illustration, and the finite dynamics and allocation examples are explanatory arguments. Their role is to expose a mechanism or boundary. They are not represented as quotations or newly discovered theorems.

The distinctions between fixed-question and question-forming inquiry, the vocabulary of conceptual moves, seven evaluative properties, hypothetical practical transformation, and preservation appendix are methodological synthesis. They draw on the source discussions while going beyond historical description.

The finite causal, similarity, error-amplification, base-rate, and amortization examples clarify conditional claims. They are constructed examples, not measurements of empirical systems.

## Limits of the source base

The collection does not claim full reading of EGA, SGA, the Tôhoku paper, FAC, original correspondence, the complete memoir, or all of Grothendieck's work. Some early English memoir routes had unusable links or extraction; no direct quotation is grounded in those failed accesses. Later reports of direct French reading have their own stated ranges and do not make every source newly inspected for this edition.

The sources overlap, and several quote the same passages. Agreement among them is not independent experimental confirmation of a discovery method. Historical attribution, mathematical validity, methodological usefulness, and success in a receiving domain remain separate questions.
